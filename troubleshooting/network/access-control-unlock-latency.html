Power and Signal Integrity Analysis: Resolving Access Control Unlock Latency

February 20, 2026
Infrastructure · Access Control · Wiegand · Cabling · Signal Integrity

Overview

A multi-door access control deployment of approximately 50 units experienced a consistent 2–3 second delay between credential presentation and door unlocking.

The system used an eMAX-LP1502 controller with HID 6005 and similar TTL/Wiegand proximity readers, magnetic locks, electric door strikes, and a regulated 12 VDC power supply.

Initial troubleshooting focused on controller configuration and relay timing. Further investigation, however, identified a wiring issue: Cat6 cable with 23–24 AWG conductors had been installed for the reader connections instead of the specified 18 AWG access-control cable.

Root Cause

Cat6 cable using 23–24 AWG conductors was installed for the TTL/Wiegand reader wiring instead of the required 18 AWG cable. The undersized conductors caused voltage drop at the reader and degraded Wiegand signal stability, resulting in delayed credential validation and a 2–3 second door-unlock latency.

Why It Matters

Access-control problems are not always caused by controller logic, software configuration, or relay timing. Power delivery and signal integrity between the controller and reader can directly affect system response.

In this deployment, the doors eventually unlocked, which made the system appear functional. However, the consistent 2–3 second delay resulted in user complaints and increased the risk of unnecessary stress on door hardware and additional maintenance costs.

The case also demonstrates why manufacturer wiring specifications should be followed consistently, particularly when the same design is deployed across many doors.

Environment

  • Deployment: Approximately 50 access-control units
  • Controller: eMAX-LP1502
  • Reader: HID 6005 and similar TTL/Wiegand proximity readers
  • Lock Devices: Magnetic locks and electric door strikes
  • Power Supply: 12 VDC regulated
  • Installed Reader Wiring: Cat6 cable — 23–24 AWG conductors
  • Required Specification: 6-conductor, 18 AWG TTL reader wiring
  • Issue Type: Power / signal integrity
  • Impact: Consistent 2–3 second door-unlock delay

Credential Flow

Understanding the credential path helped explain why reader-side power and signal integrity could affect the final unlock response.

The basic sequence is: 

Card enters active RF field
        ↓
Reader reads and decodes credential ID
        ↓
Bits are transmitted as timed LOW pulses
on D0 (0) or D1 (1), referenced to ground
        ↓
Controller reconstructs the bit stream
        ↓
Controller validates the credential
        ↓
Relay activates
        ↓
Lock releases

Improper Reader Wiring

The installed reader wiring used Cat6 cable with 23–24 AWG conductors.

Proper Reader Wiring

The specified installation uses 18 AWG access-control cable for the TTL/Wiegand reader connection.

Troubleshooting Process

1. Verify Controller Configuration

The troubleshooting process began by reviewing the access-control controller configuration and relay timing parameters.

What to look for: Verify that the controller configuration and relay timing are consistent with the expected door operation.

What it tells us: Verifying the controller configuration helps determine whether the unlock delay originates from a logical or timing configuration before investigating the physical infrastructure.

2. Measure Reader Voltage

Voltage was measured at both the panel output and the reader during badge activation.

What to look for: Compare the voltage available at the controller with the voltage reaching the reader, particularly while the reader is actively processing a credential.

What it tells us: A difference between the panel-side and reader-side measurements can indicate voltage drop along the reader wiring.

3. Review the Wiring Specification

The manufacturer wiring requirements were reviewed and compared with the cable installed in the field.

Required specification: 

6-conductor, 18 AWG TTL reader wiring

Installed wiring: 

Cat6 cable, 23–24 AWG conductors

What to look for: Compare the installed conductor size with the wiring specification required for the reader.

What it tells us: The installed Cat6 conductors did not meet the documented 18 AWG reader-wiring requirement.

4. Identify the Wiring Mismatch

The comparison confirmed that Cat6 cable with 23–24 AWG conductors had been used where 18 AWG access-control cable was required.

The smaller conductors introduced voltage drop at the reader and degraded the stability of the TTL/Wiegand signaling.

What it tells us: The repeated delay across multiple doors was associated with a common installation characteristic rather than an isolated controller or reader failure.

5. Replace the Reader Wiring and Retest

The reader wiring was replaced with 18 AWG access-control cable.

Credential presentation and door-unlock response were then tested again.

Verification: After the wiring replacement, the door responded immediately and unlock latency was confirmed at less than 500 milliseconds.

Resolution

The non-compliant Cat6 reader wiring was replaced with 18 AWG access-control cable that met the required wiring specification.

After the replacement, credential validation and door unlocking were retested. The previous 2–3 second delay was eliminated, and unlock response was confirmed at less than 500 milliseconds.

Lessons Learned

  • Manufacturer wiring specifications should be enforced consistently, especially when a design is deployed across many access-control locations.
  • Conductor size can affect voltage stability and signal integrity, even when the connected device continues to operate.
  • Single-ended TTL/Wiegand signaling depends on stable power and a stable ground reference.
  • A system that eventually performs the requested action should not automatically be considered electrically healthy; abnormal latency can be an important troubleshooting symptom.
  • Comparing measurements at the source and endpoint can help identify problems that are not visible from controller configuration alone.
  • When the same symptom appears across multiple installations, look for a common design or installation characteristic rather than assuming multiple independent device failures.
